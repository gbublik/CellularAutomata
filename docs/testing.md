# Automation tests — what each one covers

Detail behind the test-suite line in CLAUDE.md's Build / run section. Read before adding, changing or deleting a test in `Source/CellularAutomata/Private/Tests/AutomataTests.cpp`.

Nineteen tests, chosen for the places where a bug is both likely and invisible. Three cover slice capture (`Slice.Orientation` — the asymmetric-figure guard against mirroring and transposition described under `Automata/Capture/`; `Slice.Raster` — nearest-wins independent of input order, exact N×N blocks under scaling, budget refusal leaving no half-result, and empty input failing honestly rather than emitting a 1×1 file; `Slice.Tile` — the mirrored tiling turning `[0 1 2 3]` into `[0 1 2 3 2 1]`, width `2W−2`, i.e. the column past the edge must be the *penultimate* one rather than a repeat of the edge, which would lay a double line along every seam — checked on a four-colour strip, since on a flat-coloured image a mirror looks correct even when it is not). Four cover the generators (`Generation.Determinism` — same seed, same set, and a *different* seed giving a different one, since a generator that ignores the seed passes the first check perfectly; `Generation.RandomBallParity` — the golden test that `GenerateRandom()`'s refactor didn't silently shift every previously saved seed; `Generation.LatticeNeighborUniformity` — that the neighbour counts the presets promise are the counts the geometry actually has; `Generation.ParityFilter` — that the parity filter **partitions** the set rather than cuts it, i.e. `Even ∪ Odd` equals the unfiltered result *as a set* and not as a count (several generators emit the same cell more than once — `RandomBall` reject-samples with collisions — so summing lengths would prove nothing), which is what catches the trap in `FCellEmitter::Emit()` where a parity rejection must return `true`, since `false` there means overflow and would truncate the whole generator at the first node of the wrong parity; plus the closure that makes the FCC mode a lattice at all — a grid seeded on even cells must stay even *forever* under `Edges`/`EdgesFarAxes`, with `Moore` included as the counter-check that must break it, or the assertion would be vacuous). The other four: `Grid.NegativeCoords` (chunk math on negative coordinates — the floor-division trap flagged under `Automata/Grid/`, and generation is centred on the origin so negative coordinates are the norm, plus the "a re-created chunk must not inherit stale ages" guarantee), `Rules.RuleStringRoundTrip` (`Parse → Format → Parse`, range compression, every one of the 14 neighbourhood tokens surviving print-then-parse back into itself (a gap in the token table would otherwise fall through to its `M` fallback silently), and twelve malformed strings that must each be rejected *whole* with a non-empty error — including `M2` and `E2`, rejected rather than coerced), `Compute.CpuGpuParity` and `Compute.GpuBatchParity` (differential tests — they need no known-good answer, only that two implementations agree: CPU against GPU, and one batched dispatch against N single steps, comparing live cells, ages **and** decay stages). The last two are the automated form of the manual check the batching work was verified with, and they carry `EAutomationTestFlags::NonNullRHI` plus their own `GMaxRHIFeatureLevel` check so they report "skipped" rather than fail under `-nullrhi`. `Rules.NeighborhoodShells` checks the shell model itself rather than the 14 values one by one: that each shell is exactly one `d²` class with no strays from the 5×5×5 cube it is scanned from (`(2,1,0)` at `d²=5` is the one waiting to leak in), that every value equals the union of its own shells (by set algebra, since `6+8` and `12+2` both give 14 and counting alone would not notice classes swapped), that every value stays under the 26-offset ceiling, that `Moore` still returns its original offsets in the original order, that the parity properties the face-less sets exist for still hold, and that reach is 2 exactly where far axes are present. Two cover the lattice: `Lattice.OrthogonalRoundTrip` — `WorldToGrid()` must be the *exact* inverse of `GridToWorld()` at any per-axis step including an unequal Z, which is precisely the hole that existed while four hand-written divisions by `CellSize` stood in place of an inverse mapping (see `docs/lattice-and-cell-shapes.md`), plus a point taken *inside* a cell rather than at its centre landing in that cell — what the DDA pick stands on — and `WorldBoundsToCellRange()` covering every cell whose centre is in the box, asserted as a **superset** because the range is deliberately conservative while a lost cell would be a hole in the cull box's edge; `Lattice.ElongatedDodecahedronFaces` — that the twelve offsets are the actual **faces of the Voronoi cell** under a 2× Z stretch (a face towards `V` exists exactly when the midpoint to `V` is nearer the origin than any other sublattice node), which pins down both the √2 threshold and the *absence* of a face towards `(0,0,±2)`, plus BCC closure (all three coordinates of one parity, tested with `&1` rather than `%2`, which yields −1 on negatives and would silently pass), the 26-offset shader ceiling, and reach 2 — the GPU batch halo is computed from it, and an understated one silently drops border cells. `Rendering.ColorRamp` sweeps all four colour spaces against both curves and asserts that a key stop returns the key itself within 1/255: that is the only thing that catches a broken there-and-back conversion, which otherwise shows up as a barely perceptible hue shift across the *whole* ramp and is indistinguishable from an intended colour. Alongside it: nothing outside `[0,1]` and no NaN (Catmull–Rom overshoots the key range by construction, and a NaN check must come first, since comparisons against NaN are all false), the degenerate inputs the settings panel relies on (an empty ramp is "material as-is", i.e. white; a single key gives itself at any `T`), and a guard that the middle of a red→blue ramp actually *differs* between LinearRgb and Oklab — without it an implementation that ignores the space setting entirely would pass everything else. Grey sits in the key set on purpose: it has no defined hue, and that is where a naive Oklch breaks (`atan2` of zero gives an arbitrary angle). The last, `GenerationHistory`, covers the sliding window behind the HUD graph (see `Ui/` below) — and it is the reason that logic was extracted out of the orchestrator into free functions at all: the traps in it (the last sample being edited *in place* rather than appended, the rendered value being carried forward, X mapped by generation value rather than by array index) are exactly the kind that need no actor, tick or render to exercise. The normalisation added on top of it is tested the same way and for the same reason: that `P(n)/n^d` really is flat on a set that grows as a clean power, that the fitted exponent comes out exactly 3 on `n³` and returns 0 rather than a NaN when there is nothing to fit, that generation 0 is dropped from *both* series at once (leaving them the same length) and takes the window's left edge with it, and that the axis segment never collapses to zero width — it is divided by. `TrimAfter()` (the rollback behind Ctrl+Z, see `StepBackward()` in `docs/orchestrator.md`) is tested here too, and what it guards is the *asymmetry*: the samples after the rollback point go, the ones before it stay — that is the whole difference from `ResetGenerationCounter()`, which clears the graph outright because it means "a new run", and getting it wrong would silently blank the history on every press. Plus the two ends: rolling back to generation 0 leaves one sample rather than an empty graph, and trimming above the window is a no-op (a press right after loading a file, where the counter is ahead of the history).

`Editing.EditJournal` covers the *other* half of Ctrl+Z — the manual edits, which no rule can recompute and which are therefore the one thing this project does store (as deltas, see `CellEditJournal`). Three properties, each of which fails invisibly rather than loudly: that undo restores a cell **whole** — age and decay phase included, since a returned cell with age 0 flashes the wrong colour and a lost decay phase changes the *rule's* subsequent behaviour, neither of which looks like a bug in the undo; that a record holds only the cells an edit actually touched, so the journal grows with the edit rather than with the marquee (dead cells inside the selection, and cells already alive when adding, are dropped); and that replaying a journal onto a fresh seed reproduces what the live edits produced — the property the whole "recompute forward through the edits" rollback rests on, checked by running both and comparing. The trim ends are checked the same way as the graph's: an edit exactly on the rollback point survives, one past it does not.

`Sonification.Curve` covers the measurement behind the audio (see `docs/sonification.md`) — free functions over a synthetic sample window, so no actor, tick, render or audio device is involved, which is exactly why the maths was extracted out of the component in the first place. Three of the twelve sections carry the weight. **The same exponential measured with holes punched in it must give the same slope to 1e-4**: that is the whole promise that the window lives by generation number rather than array index, and it is what makes the measurement survive `StepsPerRender`, GPU batching and the fast step — the very mechanisms that make the sample series irregular. **Two histories differing by a factor of 1000 in population must agree on slope, bend and oscillation**: the promise that sound describes the *shape* of a run and not the size of the grid, without which every rule on a big grid would sound the same. And the trap: **linear growth `50·n` must classify as `Growth`, not `Saturation`** — in log space `N = a·n` has slope `1/n`, positive, decaying, with negative bend, i.e. bit-for-bit the signature of a curve levelling off, and only the fitted growth exponent (exactly 1.0 for a clean power law, well below it for something running out of steam) tells them apart. A regression there would not fail loudly; it would just quietly make every linear run sound like it was dying. Around them: the slope of a doubling-every-five-generations run coming out as `ln2/5` (units, without which every threshold would be tuned against nothing), saturation, explosive growth, collapse and oscillation each landing in their own branch — oscillation in particular asserting that "pacing on the spot" and "standing still" are *different* sounds, since a boiling equilibrium and a dead one have the same slope and differ only in path length. Then the degenerate ends, which are where silent NaN lives: extinction must leave every field finite (one `ln(0)` would poison everything downstream and the sound would go quiet forever with nothing in the log), an empty or single-sample history must report "nothing to measure" rather than a measured zero growth (the same contract as `FitGrowthExponent()`), and a window whose samples all sit on **one** generation must not divide by zero — not a hypothetical, since `NoteRendered()` edits the last sample in place and the renderer fires on every camera move with the view slice on, so a paused window really does collapse to a single X. One section checks the sample floor: at a 256-generation stride a 64-generation window catches nothing at all, and it must widen backwards instead of reporting no data while the data is right there. The last one pins `SmoothTowards()` to its semigroup property — two steps of `Tau/2` equalling one of `Tau` — which is the formal statement that the sound does not depend on how the frame time happened to be sliced, and the reason it is `1 − exp(−Dt/Tau)` rather than a `Lerp`.

Note the whole run costs an editor startup (tens of seconds) — there is no fast inner loop for UE automation tests. Deliberately not covered: anything requiring an actor, a tick or a render — that is where UE tests get expensive and brittle, and it is checked by hand in PIE instead.

`Rendering.VisibilityFilter` is the newest, and it exists because the thing it covers used to be a **copy**. The three cuts that decide what reaches the screen — cull volume, view slice, age filter — were written out twice, once in `BuildCellRenderData()` and once in `ComputeVisibleCellsBounds()`, with the invariant between them held by a comment reading "change one of the three filters — change both places". That is the kind of invariant that goes first. Both now share `CellVisibility::FFilter` (`Automata/Rendering/CellVisibilityFilter.h`), and what the test pins down is the part that is easy to get subtly wrong rather than obviously wrong: that a *disabled* slice passes everything (its `Origin`/`Forward` are left at defaults, so a dot product against them answers nothing meaningful — the guard has to come first); that the depth window really is distance ± half thickness with **inclusive** bounds, which is what both original copies did (`Depth < Min || Depth > Max`) and what decides whether a cell on the exact edge of the slice flickers; that depth is measured *along the view direction* rather than as distance to the camera, checked by moving a point far sideways and requiring it to stay in the slice — with a length instead of a dot product it would drop out, and on screen that reads as a slice bowed into a sphere; that a disabled age filter is not the same thing as "age 0 selected", since 0 is a legitimate layer (cells just born) and confusing the two would hide the most visible layer every time the filter is cleared; and that the two filters are independent, because in the loop they are two separate `continue`s ordered by cost rather than by meaning. The predicates are `FORCEINLINE` in the header on purpose — they run per cell, i.e. millions of times a frame, and an exported function across a module boundary does not inline; the `.cpp` holds only the once-per-render assembly. Being a POD, `FFilter` needs no actor, tick or render to exercise, which is the whole reason the extraction took this shape.
